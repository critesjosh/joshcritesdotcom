I've recently been helping deploy AI across organizations by streamlining existing workflows, surfacing new ones that AI makes possible, and helping developers learn complex software systems fast enough to build effectively with new tools. In every case, it is the same loop: understand the system, then act on that understanding. LLMs make both halves faster.

## Same loop, no brakes

Fundamentally, we are still building mental models and using those models to build products. A major difference is the rate at which the development loop now happens, in both learning and implementation.

Faster loops create more danger in contexts with little margin for correction: delayed feedback, low reversibility, or high consequences. When driving a car, increasing speed gives us less time to recognize and respond to an error, while the distance needed to stop grows even faster. The faster the system moves, the less room there is for feedback and judgement. We respond with speed limits, which can change as feedback and control mechanisms improve.

Velocity does not merely increase the rate of production. It increases the rate at which the state of a system changes relative to our ability to understand it. Changes interact with other changes. Errors compound. Feedback goes stale more quickly. When production outruns evaluation, risk accumulates through combinatorial explosion.

LLMs are making artifacts abundant faster than they are making judgement abundant. Historically, the cost of producing software imposed its own constraints: we could explore, build, and change only so much at once. As those constraints weaken, the bottleneck moves from production to evaluation.

These tools help us go faster, but they are agnostic about direction.

## Human speed bumps

The human role has become more consequential, not only at the executive level but also at the individual level. We are making more choices, acting on them more quickly, and propagating their consequences further. Technical and organizational debt can accumulate before feedback has time to catch up. Process friction creates waste, but it also provides involuntary deliberation time. LLMs can remove both indiscriminately.

Traditional organizational structures implicitly contain low-pass filters. Ideas take time to move through the system. Some disappear, others encounter objections, and circumstances sometimes change before action is taken. This is enormously wasteful, but it also limits the rate at which decisions propagate. Removing friction increases organizational bandwidth: good decisions travel faster, but so do bad ones. Bureaucracy is, among other things, a largely unintentional rate limiter.

Some friction does more than slow us down. The work itself forces deliberation. Writing a proposal requires articulating a model. Implementing a system exposes assumptions. Explaining something to another person reveals gaps in understanding. These are comparatively inefficient forms of cognition, but they make deliberate, clear thinking difficult to avoid. As LLMs remove this kind of work, they can also remove the thinking that happened incidentally while doing it.

As involuntary friction disappears, we may need to become more deliberate about introducing our own.

## Anti-lock brakes

Discernment, thinking, and communication help us retain control as production accelerates.

**Discernment** is deciding what deserves attention before deciding what to do about it. It involves patience, sitting with a problem and its context, and knowing when to defer judgement. It also means cultivating the ability to recognize quality, coherence, and elegance: taste.

**Thinking** is operating on what we have discerned. It means forming models, identifying assumptions, diagnosing symptoms, distinguishing evidence from inference, and comparing alternatives.

**Communication** makes those models available to a feedback system. It often means clear writing, but it can use any medium capable of transmitting thoughts, ideas, moods, emotions, or other relevant context. Communication makes a mental model observable to other people, who can question and improve it.

LLMs fit naturally within each of these processes. Allowing them to lead, however, risks premature pattern matching and the outsourcing of judgement to the model. Responsibility cannot be outsourced. LLMs can dramatically expand the space of possible interpretations, designs, and actions, but they cannot relieve us of the responsibility to choose among them.

As the space of possibilities grows, discernment and judgement become more important, not less. The challenge is no longer only how fast we can move. It is whether our capacity to notice, evaluate, and correct can keep pace.
